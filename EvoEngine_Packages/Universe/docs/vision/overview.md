# Vision and Design Pillars

[Documentation index](../README.md) | Next: [Strategic Map Design](../world/map-design.md)

Status: proposal

## Premise

Universe is a grand-strategy simulation about civilizations living under distance, delay, uncertainty, and mortality.
It takes place in a procedurally generated star cluster containing hundreds of thousands of stars. Every star maps to a
solar system with planets or other major bodies, resources, possible habitats, and a history.

The player represents a civilization rather than an immortal ruler. A game begins with that civilization seeded on a
viable or marginally viable world. The player guides it through discovery, expansion, internal change, contact,
conflict, collapse, and renewal.

The central ambition is not simply to conquer the map. It is to create a civilization, lineage, or body of knowledge
capable of surviving for as long as possible where no empire can coordinate instantly and no center remains secure
forever.

The setting draws thematic inspiration from imperial rise and collapse, exile at the periphery, preserved knowledge,
civilizational prediction, the dark forest, and the speed of light as a hard boundary. Those inspirations inform the
game's questions without prescribing its plot.

## Player Promise

The game should let the player:

- Turn one fragile inhabited world into a civilization spanning many systems.
- Make plans whose consequences take decades, centuries, or millennia to arrive.
- Explore a cluster that is neither already known nor observable in real time.
- Decide how visible, trusting, centralized, aggressive, and resilient the civilization should be.
- Watch colonies diverge as delayed autonomy becomes cultural and political independence.
- Survive disasters and imperial collapse by distributing people, industry, institutions, and knowledge.
- Lose control of regions without necessarily losing the civilization's wider legacy.
- Discover other societies through delayed light, signals, artifacts, probes, ruins, refugees, or attacks.

## Design Pillars

### Distance is gameplay

Distance determines communication delay, travel time, military response, trade cost, administrative cohesion,
observation age, and political divergence. Map design must make movement and location consequential at every scale.

### Information has a location and an age

The player sees only what the civilization could plausibly know. Reports describe the past; orders take time to become
effective; remote present state is an inference rather than a fact.

### Civilizations are processes, not solid colors

A civilization is a changing network of populations, institutions, cultures, economies, and communication links. It
can federate, centralize, fragment, merge, migrate, go dormant, or leave descendants. Borders are outcomes of reach and
relationships, not the main simulation object.

### Survival is more important than uninterrupted rule

Expansion creates strength and fragility together. Concentration supports decisive action but creates shared failure
domains. Distribution improves survival while weakening central control. Collapse reorganizes play rather than always
ending it.

### Shared causal rules govern everyone

The player, rivals, unknown societies, and splinter states use the same rules. Information cannot travel faster than
light, and physical travel is slower unless a future optional setting explicitly changes that premise.

### Scale is earned through abstraction

Hundreds of thousands of systems cannot update every person, ship, and factory in full detail. The model preserves
strategically meaningful causes, aggregates routine activity, and spends computation only where transitions occur.

## Tone and Setting

The intended tone is vast, sober, mysterious, and occasionally hopeful. The cluster should feel old enough to contain
ruins and young enough for history still to be made. Technology can become extraordinary while consequences remain
legible and physical constraints continue to matter.

At great distance the player sees ancient light. A peaceful world in a telescope may already be dead; an apparently
empty system may have changed before its observed photons arrive. The edge of the cluster provides relative isolation
and time to develop, but offers fewer resources, routes, and sources of knowledge than denser inner regions.

## Objective and End States

"Last forever" is the guiding fantasy, not a literal computable victory condition. The primary mode is an endless
chronicle that continues while the civilization has at least one viable continuation: a population, an accepted
descendant, a recoverable seed, or a durable institution capable of restarting it.

Optional horizons at 1,000, 10,000, or 100,000 simulated years can evaluate continuity, resilience, reach, vitality,
knowledge, influence, and destruction caused. No single score is mandatory, and conquest of every system is not the
only successful history.

Extinction ends direct play, but observer mode may continue so the player can learn what survived and how the
civilization is remembered.

## Early Non-Goals

- Simulating every individual person.
- Rendering every planet or ship continuously.
- Real-time tactical control of distant battles.
- Perfect scientific simulation of every represented discipline.
- Hundreds of commodities before logistics is proven strategically useful.
- Faster-than-light travel or communication in the default setting.
- Multiplayer before deterministic single-player simulation and persistence are reliable.
- A scripted recreation of any inspirational fiction.
