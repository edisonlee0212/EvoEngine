# Strategic Map Design

Status: foundational proposal

## Purpose

The map is the central strategic system. It must encourage civilizations to leave the safety of one home system,
establish durable presence across many stars, and keep extending or reorganizing that presence over long periods.
Expansion should occur because additional systems provide survival, capability, knowledge, and position, not because a
score awards colored territory for its own sake.

The map must support:

- Hundreds of thousands of physical star systems without requiring equal attention to each.
- Legible large-scale geography with recognizable regions, routes, frontiers, and directions.
- Meaningfully different inner, middle, and outer cluster strategies.
- Multiple expansion choices from most viable regions instead of one obvious route.
- Light-speed information limits and slower physical travel.
- Political fragmentation when spatial reach exceeds administrative reach.
- Histories in which territory grows, disconnects, changes identity, and reconnects.

## Core Spatial Model

The cluster can be understood as concentric annular layers around a generated center. Any star has cylindrical
coordinates `(r, theta, z)`:

- `r` is radial distance from the cluster center.
- `theta` is angular position around the center.
- `z` is height above or below the cluster's principal plane.

This creates two primary strategic directions:

1. Radial movement: inward toward denser central layers or outward toward the sparse edge.
2. Tangential movement: clockwise or counterclockwise along approximately the same annular layer.

Vertical displacement remains physically real and tactically relevant, but it is a secondary large-scale axis. It can
create bridges, bypasses, hidden approaches, and unusual local neighborhoods without making the cluster difficult to
read from its primary map view.

### Layers are a planning abstraction

Annular bands are not walls, hex rings, or discrete tracks. Stars retain continuous three-dimensional positions, and a
mission may travel directly between any physically reachable origin and destination. Bands provide regional identity,
aggregation, generation constraints, and map language.

Suggested initial hierarchy:

- Cluster: the entire playable map.
- Macro band: approximately 8 to 16 named inner-to-outer annuli.
- Sector: an angular division of a macro band.
- Neighborhood: a generated group of nearby stars connected by practical travel opportunities.
- System: one star system and its local bodies.

Band boundaries may be density-aware so each contains a useful amount of content, but the displayed radial coordinate
must remain stable and comparable across the map.

### The travel map is an emergent network

Euclidean distance alone does not define usable territory. A practical route depends on propulsion, mission mass,
energy, reliability, known hazards, support infrastructure, and acceptable travel time.

The UI may display a graph of practical connections, but those edges are recommendations or established corridors, not
magical lanes. Technology and infrastructure can turn an impractical direct route into a routine one. Hazards,
destroyed relays, political closure, or loss of knowledge can reverse that change.

## The Radial Axis: Inner and Outer

Radial direction should produce the strongest broad environmental gradient in the game.

### Moving inward

Inner layers tend to offer:

- Higher star density and more short-distance destinations.
- Greater opportunities for trade, specialization, observation, and contact.
- More route redundancy and alternative suppliers.
- Older infrastructure, ruins, established powers, or contested history in populated starts.
- Faster regional diffusion of knowledge and political events.

They also tend to impose:

- Higher detection and encounter risk.
- More competition for exceptional systems and corridors.
- Less warning time between observation and arrival.
- Greater exposure to conflicts, migration waves, and cascading failures.
- Environmental or stellar hazards appropriate to the generated cluster model.

An inward campaign is therefore a movement toward opportunity, interdependence, and disruption.

### Moving outward

Outer layers tend to offer:

- Isolation, strategic warning time, and fewer directions of approach.
- Unclaimed systems and space for independent cultural development.
- Refuge from dense regional conflicts and cascading political failure.
- Strong defensive depth when inward approaches are monitored.
- Opportunities for hidden archives, reserves, observatories, and continuity sites.

They also tend to impose:

- Longer average trips and fewer viable alternate routes.
- Smaller local markets and weaker specialization.
- Higher cost to replace a failed colony, fleet, or industrial node.
- Reduced access to discoveries, allies, and shared infrastructure.
- Greater autonomy and divergence because central assistance arrives late.

An outward campaign is therefore a movement toward resilience, independence, and logistical scarcity.

### Radial movement should remain bidirectional

The map must not imply that progress always means moving inward or always means fleeing outward. A healthy civilization
may build an outer continuity network while sending commercial or scientific branches inward. A central power may seed
the edge to survive a forecast collapse. A peripheral exile may eventually reconnect with a transformed center.

Systems and policies should make inward and outward holdings complementary rather than placing them on a single quality
ladder.

## The Tangential Axis: Along the Circle

Tangential expansion moves clockwise or counterclockwise through systems with broadly similar radial conditions. It
creates strategic choices different from crossing the radial gradient.

Tangential movement tends to provide:

- Access to more systems without immediately accepting a new radial risk profile.
- Long connected settlement belts and trade corridors.
- Rival fronts that can be approached from two directions around a band.
- Opportunities to bypass a radial barrier through a neighboring sector.
- Competition to connect separated radial spokes.
- Cultural regions shaped by shared conditions but divergent histories.

A civilization that expands only tangentially can become a long arc: economically coherent but vulnerable to being cut
into disconnected segments. A civilization that expands only radially can become a narrow spoke: environmentally
diverse and capable of linking edge to core, but exposed to interruption at a small number of bridge systems.

Combining both directions creates webs, ladders, wedges, and distributed constellations. These shapes should matter
more than the total number of claimed stars.

## Regional Topology

Perfectly uniform rings would produce repetitive decisions. Procedural structure must interrupt them with features at
several scales:

- Dense arcs: broad tangential regions with many short routes.
- Sparse gaps: costly stretches that split otherwise similar bands.
- Radial corridors: star-rich paths that connect inner and outer layers.
- Voids: volumes that create long detours or isolated pockets.
- Bridges: small sets of systems connecting major neighborhoods.
- Basins: internally connected regions with few external approaches.
- Crossroads: systems that connect radial and tangential corridors.
- Off-plane paths: three-dimensional bypasses that are hard to observe or support.

No single bridge should be required for all cluster-scale movement. Local chokepoints are strategically valuable, but
the cluster needs alternative routes so one early owner cannot permanently lock the rest of the map.

## What Territory Means

Territory is not a binary painted polygon. A civilization's spatial presence is the overlap of several fields:

- Settlement: systems containing a stable population or autonomous habitat.
- Logistics: systems that can be supplied, repaired, and reinforced within accepted limits.
- Communication: systems reached by trusted information infrastructure and standing doctrine.
- Observation: systems and routes that can be monitored with useful freshness and confidence.
- Political authority: places where institutions recognize and can execute decisions.
- Deterrence: places where credible response changes another actor's behavior.
- Cultural affiliation: populations that identify with the civilization or its lineage.

A system can be settled but politically independent, observed but unreachable, claimed but unsupported, or culturally
aligned without accepting orders. The map should display these layers separately and offer a combined summary without
pretending they are one fact.

### Operational coverage

The most useful measure of expansion is operational coverage: how many systems participate in the civilization's
survival and capability network. Empty formal claims do not count. A system contributes when it performs one or more
meaningful roles and can sustain that role under expected delay and disruption.

## Why Civilizations Expand

The game should create multiple persistent reasons to cover more systems.

### Survival and failure isolation

One system is one failure domain. Multiple separated systems can survive local ecological collapse, stellar hazards,
civil conflict, industrial accidents, or attack. Separation matters only if each site is viable enough to continue
without immediate rescue.

### Economic specialization

Systems differ in energy, matter, habitability, biosphere, position, and accumulated infrastructure. Networks of
specialized systems can support capabilities no single homeworld can efficiently maintain.

### Logistics and route construction

Relays, depots, habitats, observatories, maintenance yards, and industrial stations make longer missions practical.
Expansion creates the infrastructure required for further expansion.

### Knowledge and observation

Distributed telescopes, listening posts, probes, and archives improve warning time and reduce uncertainty. Different
radial and angular positions reveal different histories and lines of sight.

### Population pressure and political choice

Migration, ideological projects, factional separation, and local opportunity can push settlements outward even when a
central government prefers consolidation. Expansion can be a negotiated outlet for internal conflict.

### Strategic depth

Multiple routes, redundant industrial centers, reserve populations, and continuity archives make it difficult for one
strike or regional collapse to end the civilization.

### Contact and influence

Presence near signals, trade partners, ruins, rivals, and migration paths gives access to relationships that cannot be
managed effectively from a distant homeworld.

### Scarcity without forced exhaustion

The home system should not simply deplete on a timer to force expansion. Some local resources can be finite, but the
stronger pressure is opportunity cost: remaining concentrated limits redundancy, specialization, knowledge, reach, and
future options. A well-managed compact civilization should remain possible, merely exposed to different risks.

## Constraints on Expansion

Expansion should be desirable without becoming automatic exponential growth.

- Travel time delays return and reinforcement.
- Communication delay transfers real authority to local decision nodes.
- Colonies require population, tools, knowledge, and a viable path to local self-sufficiency.
- New routes increase maintenance and observation burdens.
- Long narrow networks are vulnerable to disconnection.
- Dense territory becomes visible and entangled with neighbors.
- Cultural divergence and local interests reduce central cohesion.
- Simultaneous projects compete for skilled labor and institutional capacity, not only raw material.

These pressures should change optimal spatial form. The player decides where to consolidate, where to delegate, where
to accept independence, and where to stop supporting an unsustainable branch.

## Anti-Snowball Geography

Owning more systems should increase potential without converting directly into frictionless power.

- Surplus is local until physically transported.
- Remote population cannot be commanded with current information.
- A larger perimeter creates more observation and defense obligations.
- Distant settlements may become allies, successors, or rivals rather than permanent possessions.
- Dense inner holdings gain capability while increasing contact and cascade exposure.
- Sparse outer holdings gain warning and redundancy while increasing transport cost.
- Route centralization improves efficiency but creates critical failure points.

An old civilization may be large yet loosely coordinated. A smaller civilization may act faster locally. Expansion
changes the type of power a civilization holds rather than only increasing a global multiplier.

## Edge Exile as a Map Scenario

A peripheral start embodies the game's core tradeoff. The player begins near the outer edge with few nearby rivals and
more uninterrupted time to build institutions. Expansion initially has three recognizable choices:

1. Extend tangentially to build a safe outer arc and diversify local systems.
2. Build a radial spoke inward toward denser resources, information, and eventual contact.
3. Do both slowly, accepting higher cost to avoid dependence on one corridor.

The interior is not merely an endgame destination. Signals, refugees, ruins, trade opportunities, and threats can move
outward while the player's missions move inward. The history of the center arrives first as old light, then as reports,
then perhaps as physical consequences.

## Map Generation Process

A candidate generation pipeline is:

1. Generate the continuous three-dimensional stellar density field and cluster center.
2. Place stars deterministically and derive stable cylindrical coordinates.
3. Define macro bands and angular sectors for aggregation and identity.
4. Generate correlated density, resource, habitability, hazard, and historical fields.
5. Shape dense arcs, radial corridors, voids, bridges, basins, and off-plane bypasses.
6. Generate detailed systems lazily from per-system sub-seeds.
7. Build practical route graphs for several representative propulsion and infrastructure levels.
8. Place civilization seeds, ruins, and landmarks using travel-time and observation-time separation.
9. Analyze reachability, redundancy, bottlenecks, opportunity, and encounter timing.
10. Reject invalid candidates or retain unusual viable asymmetry.

Physical properties should create geography where possible. Gameplay analysis may tune distributions, but it should not
silently add arbitrary impassable borders.

## Map Interface Requirements

The cluster view should expose structure without drawing every relationship simultaneously.

Required overlays include:

- Radial bands, angular sectors, and named regions.
- Star density and practical travel-time contours.
- Established routes, candidate routes, and route vulnerability.
- Settlement, logistics, communication, observation, authority, and cultural presence.
- Information age and confidence.
- Known resources, hazards, signals, ruins, and other civilizations.
- Earliest arrival cones for messages and physical missions.
- Historical territory and route state at a selected known time.

The player should be able to select a system or region and ask, "What can reach this place, through which routes, by
when, and according to whose knowledge?"

Radial and tangential language should appear in summaries: inward frontier, outer arc, clockwise neighbor, radial
corridor, and similar terms make a huge cluster easier to remember than coordinates alone.

## Prototype Metrics

Map quality should be measured before expensive civilization simulation is added. For generated seeds, record:

- Stars and viable settlement candidates per band and sector.
- Median and tail travel times to nearby useful systems.
- Number of independent early expansion directions from each seed.
- Radial and tangential reach at representative technology levels.
- Corridor redundancy and the impact of losing the most central system.
- Distribution and severity of local chokepoints.
- Expected time to first remote observation, message, and physical contact.
- Systems reachable before the home system faces a modeled failure horizon.
- Difference between formal claims and operationally supported coverage.
- Connectivity of edge arcs and number of viable edge-to-inner spokes.

## First Map Prototype

The first prototype should be headless and use only stars, distances, simple route costs, abstract system values, and one
expanding agent. It should test whether the geography creates recognizable radial and tangential choices before adding
planet detail, rendering, diplomacy, or combat.

Exit criteria:

- A 100,000-star seed can be generated reproducibly within an agreed memory and time budget.
- Starts in the edge, middle, and inner bands each have at least two viable but meaningfully different expansion shapes.
- A peripheral start can form both an outer arc and an inward spoke without either being universally superior.
- Removing a local bridge changes routes without disconnecting most of the cluster permanently.
- Expansion value arises from concrete system roles rather than a territory-count reward.
- Automated summaries can explain why an agent selected radial, tangential, or consolidating actions.

## Open Questions

1. Should macro bands have equal radial width, roughly equal star counts, or density-adaptive strategic depth?
2. How many bands and sectors remain readable at cluster scale?
3. How thick should the cluster be, and when does vertical geography become a primary strategic axis?
4. Which astrophysical gradients should be physically modeled, and which gameplay gradients are setting assumptions?
5. How permissive is direct travel compared with travel through established infrastructure?
6. Can a compact one-system civilization remain viable indefinitely, even if strategically vulnerable?
7. How should uninhabited support infrastructure affect settlement and territory summaries?
8. How does procedural naming make arcs, spokes, bridges, and basins memorable?
9. Which map properties may be tuned after generation without making seeds feel artificially balanced?
10. How should the map show disputed and observer-relative territory without visual overload?
